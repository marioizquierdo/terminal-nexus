# Where the campaign starts

**Document role:** Four ways to open the campaign, compared; plus the "lose the first battle" idea
**Status:** Draft for Mario to react to — this is Decision A, and Decision D
**Updated:** 2026-08-28
**License:** Story material is CC BY-SA 4.0; the analysis is Apache-2.0

You have already decided the shape: Citizens first, the Ravels as the enemy, the story kicked off by
the pyramid waking up. So the open question is not *which factions* — it is **which moment of that
story the player is dropped into**, and what they are doing in it.

Four answers below. They are genuinely different, not variations, and two of them would change what
the first hour of the game feels like.

---

## Opening 1 — The raid arrives

The pyramid rose fourteen hours ago and destroyed the research annex. You are what walked out: two
small squads, one fabricator, and a perimeter that mostly exists on paper. A Ravel raiding party is
coming down off a ridge to the north-west. Hold the line and keep your workers alive.

**What the player does:** places a few units, spends a small budget, picks one upgrade, then watches
the raid arrive and break — or not.

**Why it teaches well.** In an autobattler, the only decisions that exist are the ones made *before*
the fight. Defending is the situation that puts the most weight on exactly those decisions: where
things stand, and whether the workers are behind something. Attacking teaches movement and army
composition, which are also real, but they are the lessons a player can pick up by watching. Placement
is the one they have to be made to care about.

**What it costs to build:** essentially nothing new. Every unit it needs already exists in the test
content, and the fight it describes is the kind of fight the engine has already been proven on.

**Its weakness, and it is real.** "Hold the line against the first wave" is the most common opening in
the genre. The game's actual subject — an enormous machine that woke up and started handing people
equipment — sits in the background of a fight while the player learns where to put a marksman.

---

## Opening 2 — The pyramid wakes up, and nothing attacks you

No enemy army in the first mission. The pyramid rises. What comes out of it is an ancient maintenance
machine going about its function: it is not hostile to anyone, it does not care that you are there,
and it will walk through your camp and kill everything in its path because that is where its path
goes. You survive it. You do not defeat it.

Then the Ravels arrive in mission two, and a war is almost a relief.

**Why it is tempting.** It is the only opening whose first mission *is* the game's premise. "You
cannot steer the fight, you can only prepare for it" is much easier to feel when the thing coming at
you is indifferent rather than hostile — nothing is targeting you, and you still have to have built
the right thing in the right place. It is also the most distinctive opening available. Nothing else in
the genre starts like this.

**What it costs to build.** The most of the four, and the costs are real rather than fussy:

- A neutral third party. Everything the engine knows about belongs to one side or the other; a thing
  that belongs to nobody and attacks whatever is in front of it is new behaviour, not a reskin.
- A way to win a mission that has no opponent. Right now a fight ends when one side's base is
  destroyed, one side is wiped out, or the clock runs out — and running out the clock is currently a
  draw, not a victory. "Survive the thing" needs an answer to that, and it is a change to a rule the
  rest of the game rests on.
- It spends the campaign's best late card early. An indifferent ancient machine is the strongest
  single idea in the whole setting, and using it in mission one means mission six has to find
  something bigger.

---

## Opening 3 — You lose the first battle

This is Decision D, and it gets its own section below because it is a different kind of question.
Short version: a prologue at the annex, at full strength — a real base, a real army, real upgrade
choices — and partway through the mission the pyramid rises *through your base* and destroys it. You
lose the map to the ground, not to an enemy. Everything after that is the rebuild.

---

## Opening 4 — First contact is small and strange

The war has not started. A survey outpost has gone quiet, and you are sent with a handful of units to
find out why. What you find is a Ravel scavenging crew stripping it — and they run rather than fight.
No base, almost no build phase, five minutes long.

**Why it is tempting.** It is the gentlest possible on-ramp, and it makes the first real mission (the
raid) land harder, because the player has already met these people and they were not frightening.

**Why I would not open with it.** A first mission with almost no build phase teaches almost nothing
about the game — and the build phase is the game. It is a good *second* mission, or a good five-minute
prologue, but as the opening it postpones the thing the player came for.

## Recommendation for Decision A

**Build Opening 1 as the first real mission. Put a two-minute version of Opening 2 in front of it as
an opening scene. Keep Opening 4 as mission two's shape if you want it. Treat Opening 3 as Decision D.**

The reasoning, in the order it actually weighs:

**1. The first mission you build should be the one that teaches the build phase best, because you are
also using it to learn your own game.** You said the campaign is partly there to help you understand
and polish the mechanics, one at a time. A defence mission puts every one of your unproven mechanics
under load in a legible way — placement, worker safety, production, one upgrade choice — with nothing
new underneath it. Opening 2 would have you debugging a new kind of enemy at the same time as you are
deciding whether the build phase is any good.

**2. Opening 2's real asset is not the machine — it is that the player meets the machine before they
meet a war. And you can have that for the price of one scene.** Two lines of text and a picture: a
seismic alert that gets reclassified as a musical tone, then the pyramid, drawn at a size that does
not fit on screen. Then the raid mission starts. You get the distinctiveness without the neutral-enemy
engineering.

**3. There is a version of that scene that is worth much more and costs much more, and you should know
it exists.** Run the opening scene on a *different interface* — plain colonial survey chrome, no
mention of a "Pulse" or a "Build Phase" or an "Operator", because those words do not exist yet. Then
the pyramid rises and the interface changes, and the vocabulary the whole rest of the game uses
arrives at that instant, unexplained. It plants the campaign's biggest question in the first minute
without saying a word about it. It also means building a second look for the game that appears exactly
once, which is expensive. File it as an ambition, not a plan.

**What would change my mind.** If distinctiveness matters more to you than build cost right now,
Opening 2 is the honest answer and I would not argue hard against it. Its problem is engineering and
pacing, not the idea — the idea is better than mine.

## Decision D — the "big battle, then you lose everything" opening

You asked me to explore this properly. Here is what I found.

### What the trope actually needs to work

Its engine is **contrast**, and contrast has a precondition that most implementations get wrong: the
player has to have *used* the power, not just been shown it. Metroid Prime hands you the full suit and
lets you fight with it for ten minutes before stripping it. Homeworld lets you spend a whole mission
believing in your fleet before it brings you home to a planet that is gone. Into the Breach solves the
same problem for free by making the loss part of the premise — you are the survivors of a timeline
that was already lost, so nothing has to be taken from you on screen.

So it needs: real power, really used, taken away for a reason the player reads as **not their fault**,
and a rebuild whose smallness is legible against the memory of the first mission.

And it has one specific, nasty failure mode. A mission you are meant to lose that *feels* like a
mission you failed is worse than no prologue at all. The player's first experience of your game
becomes "I did something wrong and I do not know what."

### Three places it could go

**As a prologue, before everything.** This fits the story exactly — the pyramid destroying the annex
is already the thing that happened before mission one starts, so you would be playing the scene the
rest of the campaign is the aftermath of. It is also the natural home for the changing-interface idea
above, because the vocabulary can arrive at the moment the pyramid does.

But it needs a full-strength army invented for one mission and never used again — which is exactly the
roster work you have been deliberately putting off — plus scripted terrain destruction, plus a defeat
that the game treats as an outcome rather than a failure. It is the most expensive mission in the
campaign and it would be the first one you build.

**Folded into the first mission, for free.** Do not build a prologue at all. Just start the raid
mission with the annex's wreckage and salvage already lying on the map, and let the briefing carry the
rest. Costs a sentence. The catch is that the player never held the big army, so there is no contrast
— you are telling them they lost something instead of taking it from them.

**After the opening arc, as the turn into the next one.** The player spends six missions accumulating
units, structures and upgrades. Then a mission takes all of it away. The big army is the one they
actually built, over hours, by their own decisions — so the contrast is *earned* rather than granted,
which is strictly stronger than the prologue version on the trope's own terms.

It is also far cheaper: no invented army, no throwaway content, and the campaign already needs to be
able to say "you have this, but not on this map" for ordinary mission design reasons.

### Recommendation for Decision D

**Design it now, put it after the first six missions, and build it last.**

The after-the-arc version is better than the prologue version *and* costs a fraction as much, which is
a rare combination and worth taking. Keep the prologue idea alive as an ambition — mostly for the
changing-interface trick rather than for the spectacle — and if it ever gets built, build it once
several missions have shipped. It is the mission that most depends on tools that do not exist yet, and
the one where getting it wrong damages the first ten minutes of your game rather than the middle.

The free version (wreckage on the ground in mission one) costs nothing and can go in whenever the
first mission is authored.
