# Who leads, and what happens to them

**Document role:** The commander's identity, how the story escalates, and where a second commander fits
**Status:** Draft for Mario to react to — this is Decision B, and Decision E

Everything below is one proposal. The names are placeholders and the easiest thing here to change.

## 1. The story, in as few words as possible

You asked for conceptual and short. So, no new prose — just spines.

**The campaign in one line:**

> Something enormous woke up and started issuing us equipment. We took it.

**The arc in three words: recognised, then equipped, then enrolled.** The first mission is the machine
noticing you. The middle is it handing you things. The end is the discovery that accepting them was
joining something with its own purposes.

**Six missions, one line each.** Working titles only — they came out of an earlier drafting pass and
are free to change.

| # | Working title | The whole mission, in a sentence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | PERIMETER | Hold the fence. The pyramid is watching the fight. |
| 2 | RIGHT OF SALVAGE | Take the wreckage. The machine already knew whose it was. |
| 3 | RESTORATION | Your commander dies mid-fight, and the machine files it as a scheduling matter. |
| 4 | PRECOMMITTED | One of your decisions was made for you. It was a cautious one. |
| 5 | TWELVE OF TWELVE | The report says everyone came back. You watched them not come back. |
| 6 | ANNEX ZERO | Something older arrives, hostile to nobody, and the site stops being findable. |

## 2. Who the commander is — Decision B

The name matters far less than **what she did for a living before the pyramid woke up**, because that
choice decides what the campaign is about and how she sounds in every line she ever gets.

Four candidates:

**A line officer.** Career military, holds the site under emergency powers, never asked for any of
this. Doctrine: dig in, confirm, then move. Safe, immediately legible, and a perfect fit for a first
mission about holding a perimeter. The risk is that she is the default — a competent soldier in a
science-fiction war is a character the player has met many times.

**The survey lead.** A scientist who spent fourteen years studying the buried ruin without ever
learning how old it was, and the thing picks *her*. Wonderful irony: she knows the most about it and
still knows nothing, and her ignorance is the player's ignorance, so the game never has to pretend the
protagonist understands more than you do. The problem is mechanical — the commander is a unit that
stands on the front line and fights, and a career archaeologist doing that takes explaining.

**The construction foreman.** The person who was building the annex when it stopped existing.
Doctrine: build through it. This is the most *Citizen* answer available — the faction's whole identity
is pragmatic builders and ordinary people being brave inside procedures, and the game's core loop is
literally building and then hoping. Same mechanical wrinkle as the scientist: a foreman as a frontline
fighter needs a reason.

**The junior officer.** Everyone senior died in the annex, and command falls to whoever is left.
Gives the biggest growth arc across six missions. Also the most generic starting point.

### Recommendation

**Merge the first and the third: make her an engineering officer.** Military rank, engineering corps,
commands this perimeter because she is the one who built it.

Three reasons:

1. **She does the player's job.** The player's entire contribution is placing things and then watching.
   A commander whose profession is the same thing means every order she gives is about the activity
   the player is actually doing, and the faction's "we are builders, not warriors" identity arrives
   through a person instead of a description.
2. **It solves the frontline problem for free.** An engineering officer standing in the middle of her
   own fortifications is not strange. A scientist doing it is.
3. **It keeps the safe option's virtues.** She is still military, still legible in mission one, still
   able to say soldier things without the game having to explain her.

**And keep the survey lead as a second character who does not command.** Split the two jobs: the
commander decides things, the scientist wonders what the thing is. That way the commander never has to
deliver exposition — she can stay dry and practical, which is much better writing — and the campaign
still has a voice asking the questions the player is asking. It costs nothing: a recurring name and a
few lines per mission.

If you want the existing name, Edda Vasse is a good name and works fine for this job. The decision I
am asking for is the job, not the name.

## 3. How she gets introduced

**She is introduced by being ordinary, and becomes a commander at the end of the first mission.**

- **In the briefing**, she is a fact, not a character. She holds the ground with what walked out of the
  annex: two squads and a fabricator. The player is not asked to care yet.
- **During the fight**, she is a strong unit walking around. The mechanic that makes commanders special
  — that they die, are gone for a while, and come back — exists and works, but the first mission does
  not use it and should not kill her. That beat belongs to mission three, where it is the whole point.
- **In the debrief**, she becomes something else, in two sentences: she accepts the connection to the
  machine, and her first standing order is that nobody thanks the pyramid.

That last line is the actual introduction. It arrives late on purpose, and the character shows up fully
formed in eight words: dry, faintly hostile toward the thing that chose her, and funny. The mechanical
fact — she can now come back from death — and the characterisation land at the same instant, which is
the cheapest possible way to make a mechanic feel like a story beat.

Her first line in combat is three words of character and also, quietly, the campaign's thesis: *"By the
book. The new book."*

## 4. How the war with the Ravels escalates

The mistake available here is escalating by *quantity* — wave two is bigger than wave one. That gets
boring by mission three and it means every escalation has to be balanced.

The better version escalates by **what the enemy wants**, which costs nothing mechanically and changes
the meaning of every mission:

| # | What they are after | What that does to the player |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Your ground.** They are testing the fence. Banter, a probe, an orderly withdrawal | Establishes the war as ordinary, which is what makes everything after it land |
| 2 | **Your wreckage.** Not the position — the material. They want what things are made of | The first reframe: this war is about scrap, and both sides are scavenging the same corpses |
| 3 | **Your commander.** They come for her specifically, and they get her | Ground, then material, then a named person. That is the honest ladder |
| 4 | **Authorship.** The enemy is no longer only them. Something has started making your decisions | The antagonist stops being a person. Your own tools are participants now |
| 5 | **Nothing.** They have gone quiet. What is wrong is your own reporting | The war becomes a question about what you can trust. Nothing is attacking and something is very wrong |
| 6 | **Neither of you matters.** They show up to talk. Both armies are standing in front of something that does not acknowledge either | The war was never the point, and both sides find that out together |

Six steps, each a change in *kind* rather than degree. Nothing on that list requires an enemy that is
mechanically stronger than the last one, which matters a lot for a game with no balance data yet:
escalation that lives in intent is free to tune.

## 5. A second commander — Decision E

You have a good cast sketched already and nothing needs inventing, only placing.

The constraint that decides it: **a mission where the player has a different commander is teaching two
things at once** — its own new idea, and "your commander changed." If every mission gets exactly one
new idea (see [`unlocking-and-teaching.md`](unlocking-and-teaching.md)), then all six of the opening
arc's slots are already spoken for, and inserting a commander swap means demoting one of them.

Four ways it could go:

**A rival Citizen officer, as a voice at mission four and playable later.** Someone who believes
security is not a phase of the emergency but the permanent condition — the faction's own contradiction
wearing a uniform. At mission four she turns up as a human authority who *approves your build*, which
rhymes beautifully with that mission's premise that something else has started making your decisions:
two entities choosing for you, and only one of them can be argued with. Costs a voice and one
restriction in the build phase. No units, no roster.

Then she opens the next arc as a playable commander: you *are* her, you may build anything, you may
advance nothing, and you win by finishing with a bigger perimeter than you started. The lesson is the
wall as a strategy — and the discovery that it works, which is the uncomfortable part.

**The Ravel leader, playable for one mission.** You learn the enemy's rules by using them. Enormous
teaching value, enormous cost — it means building out a second faction's whole package before the
first one is settled.

**A different Ravel antagonist.** Someone whose objective is not to kill you but to bring every
pyramid down, his own included. Cheapest of the lot: a second enemy schedule and no new player
content. Teaches that an opponent can want something other than your death, which nothing else in the
arc does.

**Nobody until the opening arc ships.** Free, and defensible. Costs the campaign its only chance to
make the faction's internal argument playable rather than described.

### Recommendation

**The rival officer — as a voice at mission four, and playable at the start of the next arc.** She
gives the player the character six missions before they play her, at almost no content cost, and puts
the actual commander swap somewhere it does not have to compete with a teaching lesson.

The alternate Ravel antagonist is a cheap addition inside the arc if a mission wants an enemy with a
different goal. Playing as the Ravels stays available and should wait until the Citizens' own set of
units is settled.
