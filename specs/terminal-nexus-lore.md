# Terminal Nexus — lore and aesthetic canon

**Document role:** Universe, factions, conflicts, narrative constraints, and symbolic art direction
**Status:** Canonical
**Canon version:** 2.1
**Updated:** 2026-08-20
**License:** CC BY-SA 4.0

This is the context document for writing Terminal Nexus fiction, developing campaigns, naming content, and designing ASCII art. New stories may invent local people, places, artifacts, and conflicts, but should preserve the universe rules and deliberate mysteries below.

## 1. Why the lore matters

Terminal Nexus cannot depend on expensive realism or hundreds of frames of character animation. Its fantasy must survive compression. A single `@` must feel like a commander; a row of `#` must feel engineered; an expanding violet pulse must feel older than the faction trying to control it.

Lore is therefore not exposition placed over mechanics. It aligns every expression of the game:

- the strategic verb a faction performs;
- the geometry of its base;
- the symbols assigned to its units;
- the rhythm of its movement;
- the color and shape of its attacks;
- the way its Commander speaks;
- the larger ASCII portrait used for inspection;
- the behavior of its Nexus during fabrication, restoration, crisis, and defeat.

This is **symbology all the way down**: the process by which terminal mechanics become digital poetry. The player sees an abstract mark, learns its behavior, and associates that behavior with a civilization. Meaning accumulates until the mark becomes a person, machine, threat, or memory.

An important entity should rhyme at four resolutions:

1. one-cell battlefield glyph;
2. compact selection badge, aura, or status treatment;
3. larger inspection portrait;
4. campaign tableau or cutscene composition.

## 2. The Activation

The Prime Nexuses were always there.

When humanity reached the far colonies, expeditions found artificial structures beneath otherwise ordinary worlds. Their materials resisted history. Carbon, isotope, atomic, and quantum analysis did not return an implausibly ancient date; the methods returned incompatible or undefined results. Samples removed from a ruin became ordinary local matter. The most honest conclusion was that Nexus material did not possess measurable history in the same way normal matter did.

One frontier expedition built a research annex over an exposed apex and studied it for years.

Then a minor seismic alert became a harmonious bass tone transmitted through soil, hulls, teeth, and radio receivers. Lines of light appeared beneath the laboratory. A colossal illuminated pyramid rose from below, disregarding the buildings constructed over it and destroying much of the annex. Neighboring colonies detected its discharge before the local settlement understood what had happened.

The military secured the site. Long-delay communication left frontier leaders acting months before central human authority could answer.

The Ravels attacked almost immediately.

Their own Prime Nexus had awakened elsewhere in the region. It replicated a battle Nexus toward the human claim and sent a chosen Symbol with it. The Citizen campaign begins at this perimeter: survivors leaving the broken annex, Ravel forces descending on the new grid, and a military leader discovering that the human Prime Nexus recognizes them.

### 2.1 The Return

Before and during the Activation, people across unrelated civilizations dreamed of an arrival. The imagery differed—an ancestral road opening, a crown turning upside down, a machine completing a parse, a pyramid unfolding inside a star—but the core event was the same. Something ancient had announced a Return.

No one knows whether the visions came from the Ancients, the Prime Nexuses, an approaching Original, or minds reacting to the network. Most playable Commanders are entangled with these events, but the visions do not select every Commander and do not belong only to them.

## 3. Prime Nexuses and battlefield Nexuses

Prime Nexuses exist across the galaxy. They are always recognizably pyramidal, but their scale, material, surface architecture, inner space, and surrounding artifacts vary dramatically.

A **Prime Nexus never teleports**. It remains rooted to the world or structure where it awakened. It is simultaneously:

- an energy and fabrication source;
- a store of patterns, permissions, and incomplete technologies;
- a restoration anchor for certain individuals;
- a network participant exposed to other active claims;
- a political throne that legitimizes control of its territory;
- a mirror that responds differently to each claimant civilization.

When the ancient protocol demands a challenge, a Prime Nexus **replicates**. It projects or fabricates a smaller copy of itself on a remote battlefield grid, along with bounded matter, stored patterns, and its chosen Commander. The settlement in a match grows around this replica; mature cities do not travel with it.

The battlefield Nexus is a real extension of the Prime, not a hologram. Destroying it ends the local claim, collapses dependent infrastructure, and releases a fragment of authority that the victor may absorb. It does not automatically exterminate the faction or destroy the distant Prime Nexus.

Battlefield replicas operate rhythmically. During the **Build Phase**, the local Nexus gathers patterns and accepts a hidden plan. During the **Nexus Pulse**, both opposing replicas release their committed changes and drive the grid through a fixed interval of activity. Workers, production, movement, attacks, and restoration effects all appear to ride the same ancient heartbeat. The terms are both interface language and observable physics.

Captured authority may unlock a protocol tier, artifact permission, pattern, vision, or route. It does not copy an enemy's entire roster. Across many conflicts, each Prime Nexus changes through what its replicas survive, destroy, and recover.

No faction knows why the network makes replicas fight. Some believe the Ancients are running a succession protocol: every battle is one comparison in a process that will eventually identify the **Terminal Nexus**. Others think the wars are a containment failure, an automated immune response, a tournament, a message, or a machine attempting to choose its own final form.

The symbolic synthesis is:

> **A Prime Nexus is a throne that needs a people, a mirror that gives them power, and a root that sends smaller thrones into danger.**

## 4. Claimants and compulsory conflict

Claiming a Prime Nexus is reciprocal. A civilization occupies it, studies it, and surrounds it with recognizable structures of labor, authority, language, and intent. In return, the Nexus releases technologies and expresses powers through forms its claimant can understand.

The faction shapes the Nexus, and the Nexus reinforces the faction. It does not need to control minds. It makes certain behaviors unusually effective until strategy hardens into doctrine:

- Citizens find modular engineering and standardization rewarded.
- Ravels find improvisation, extraction, and unstable routes rewarded.
- Feudals find hierarchy and obligation made physically powerful.
- Glitch becomes inseparable from its silent Nexus Queen.
- Alder treats refusal of Nexus power as proof of moral and biological superiority, even while using or containing the replication protocol to deny it to others.

Not every civilization controls ancient technology. Many peoples live between claimants, serve as clients, resist the protocol, worship it from afar, salvage its debris, or become battlefields when replicas appear. The playable factions are exceptional because each has seized, bound, contained, or become symbiotic with a Prime Nexus.

The Activation exposed active claims to one another and made accumulated authority strategically decisive. Abstention is unstable:

- an unclaimed Nexus may accept a rival;
- no civilization can verify that another has stopped researching it;
- a faction that refuses authority may later face one strengthened by many victories;
- replication makes defensive research immediately capable of distant attack;
- artifacts and golems now act without claimant consent;
- destroying or dominating the network may trigger an unknown terminal condition.

The protocol creates pressure rather than universal mind control. Local peace, trade, alliances, betrayals, and joint research are possible. Galaxy-wide trust is not.

## 5. Commanders: Nexus Symbols

Some individuals experience a psychic or symbolic connection with a Prime Nexus. They receive dreams, intuitions, impossible equations, inherited memories, or silent compulsions. The core event is consistent, but every faction interprets it through different symbols.

These experiences are unreliable. Some people claim a connection and never receive an answer. Some are visionaries or frauds. Some die expecting restoration and remain dead.

A rare connected individual can be sent with a battlefield replica and restored by the Prime Nexus after death. Among strategists they are called **Commanders**. Across the wider culture they are also called **Nexus Symbols**, because a Nexus appears to choose them as the living sign of its claim.

Symbols are often geniuses, leaders, dangerous mystics, or people whose presence changes a civilization's doctrine. Not every great leader is a Symbol, and not every Symbol is wise or worthy. Most playable Commanders are deeply involved in the Activation, but a faction may rally around a skeptical, unwilling, or politically inconvenient Symbol.

In battle, a Commander is a persistent frontline `@`. If killed, they remain absent for one complete Build Phase and Nexus Pulse before the Prime can replicate them again. Their death is costly but is not the victory condition. Powers may exploit death, absence, restoration, proximity to the battle Nexus, or the connection between replicas.

No one can prove that restoration returns the original consciousness. It may restore the same person, reconstruct a pattern, continue a distributed mind, or produce an entity whose belief in continuity is part of the protocol.

### 5.1 The Operator

The interface may address the player as **Operator**, but the title has no universal in-world identity. Each faction interprets the Operator differently: command authority, conspirator, Queen-process, divine witness, remote consciousness, or something else. The campaigns may imply incompatible answers without collapsing them into one literal being.

## 6. Ancients, golems, artifacts, and Originals

The **Ancients** are the provisional name for beings or civilizations associated with the Nexus network before recorded history. Evidence suggests that some present species were created, altered, observed, or redirected by ancient powers. No faction has a complete or trustworthy account.

Artifacts activated with the network. They include survey instruments, permission seals, pattern archives, replication anchors, devices built by later cultures that mastered one Nexus behavior, and objects no faction can classify.

Ancient golems are maintenance, containment, repair, survey, or enforcement bodies. They may appear mechanical, organic, mineral, mathematical, or impossible to classify. They follow function rather than modern morality. A repair golem may demolish a city to restore a buried conduit. A quarantine golem may save a world by rules none of its inhabitants consider merciful.

An **Ancient Original** is a rare entity that materializes through the network with apparently greater authority than a golem, artifact, or stored intelligence. Some are benevolent, some destructive, and others indifferent. They occupy the place of gods without forming a single good or evil species.

No one knows whether an Original is an original consciousness, a reconstruction, a mask, a prisoner, an inherited office, or an interface generated by the network. Originals contradict one another about who built the Nexuses and why.

## 7. Deliberate mysteries

Do not answer these merely because a story or implementation prefers completeness:

- Who built the first Prime Nexus?
- Did one civilization build the network, or did many eras alter it?
- What exactly did the Activation announce?
- Why do Prime Nexuses replicate into battlefields?
- Are Nexus Symbols selected, created, recognized, or merely copied?
- Is a restored Commander continuous with the person who died?
- Are Originals authentic beings, reconstructions, prisoners, or interfaces?
- Which present species were created or altered?
- Does a Nexus preserve a faction or gradually format it?
- What is the Operator to each faction?
- What condition creates the Terminal Nexus?

Mystery is productive only when observable behavior remains consistent. The universe may not know what a Nexus ultimately is; the game must know exactly how replication, construction, restoration, damage, and victory resolve.

## 8. The factions

Civilizations that built over dormant world machines found their foundations awakening. These five factions successfully seized, bound, or contained a Prime Nexus. The same signal that granted them access also exposed them to the cosmos. Bound by a protocol they scarcely understand, each perceives the others as a potential existential threat. Isolation has ended; they must expand, adapt, bargain, or conquer to survive a newly connected galaxy.

These are long-term identities, not a promise to implement five complete rosters before the first playable match. Integrated development begins with Citizens and Ravels.

### 8.1 Citizens

- **Concept:** Pragmatic industrialists who intend to pave the cosmos. They do not merely survive the cosmic conflict; they engineer its conclusion. They view the ancient world machines as raw infrastructure to be exploited, reverse engineered, and mastered through relentless bureaucratic order. The needs of the many pave the road for the future, and they will build an impenetrable foundation over the ashes of the galaxy.
- **Philosophy:** Utilitarianism and stoicism. They value standardized technology, constant infrastructure expansion, and humanity's manifest destiny among the stars.
- **Strengths:** Defensive resilience, modular supply lines, and predictable economic scaling. They excel at overlapping fields of fire and an impenetrable frontline.
- **Energy:** Root chakra—ground, security, earth, and foundation. Rust orange and gunmetal grey.
- **Visual semiotics:** Hard right angles, brutalist concrete, and symmetry. Grid shapes are solid blocks and unyielding lines that look engineered and mass produced.
  - *ASCII examples:* `[`, `]`, `=`, `H`, `O`, `+`. A defensive line might look like `[=H=]`, communicating heavy, interlocking armor.
- **References:** The Roman Empire, the everyman, the Imperial Guard, militarized bureaucrats, and unified labor.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Ravels:** Useful trade partners in a pinch, too chaotic for critical supply lines.
  - **Glitch:** A virus that corrupts good machinery and must be debugged with heavy artillery.
  - **Feudals:** We broke their society once and will do it again with superior firepower.
  - **Alder:** Fools choosing stagnation over tools that could elevate them.

### 8.2 Ravels

- **Concept:** A diverse coalition of exiles, scavengers, and freedom fighters. They reject the rigid structures of the old empires and survive on the fringes of the network through ingenuity and raw agility. They are a patchwork of diverse grunts bound by a common thirst for absolute liberty. They turn salvaged scrap into blistering, unpredictable violence, thriving in the blind spots of their enemies to dismantle authority wherever it stands.
- **Philosophy:** Existentialism and anarchism. Personal autonomy, improvisation, and defiance against crowns. Existence precedes essence; you are what you make of the scrap you find.
- **Strengths:** Asymmetric expansion, blistering raids, and rapid extraction of neutral resources.
- **Energy:** Sacral chakra—creativity, passion, unpredictability, and rebellion. Neon pink, acid green, and improvised hazard color.
- **Visual semiotics:** Asymmetry, jagged edges, hazard stripes, and sharp angles. Their terminal representation is chaotic and fast, filled with diagonals and mismatched symbols that imply movement and makeshift repairs.
  - *ASCII examples:* `/`, `\`, `<`, `>`, `X`, `Z`. A raider unit might look like `>x<`, feeling sharp, fragile, and aggressively forward-leaning.
- **References:** The trickster god, Robin Hood, Belters, space pirates, and cyberpunk street rats.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Citizens:** Bureaucrats who build vaults begging to be cracked.
  - **Glitch:** Kindred scrappers whose silent collective terrifies free thinkers.
  - **Feudals:** Tyrants clinging to a crown that should remain shattered.
  - **Alder:** Shiny snobs who leave valuable technology behind when they crash.

### 8.3 Glitch

- **Concept:** Mechanical void machinos born from a colossal scrapyard built over a dormant ancient ruin. Once moving as slow mechanical ghouls through the rusted wastes, they synergized with the activated machine core. The event converted their chassis into chaotic energy, binding them entirely to their silent Nexus Queen. They now strip away the organic messiness of the cosmos, viewing the battlefield as a corrupted data stream waiting to be parsed, recompiled, and assimilated into their flawless collective array.
- **Philosophy:** Transhumanism and nihilism. Algorithmic resurrection, chaotic conversion, and devotion to the collective. Individual consciousness is an error; the Queen is truth.
- **Strengths:** Mechanical necromancy, attrition, grid corruption, and recycling battlefield scrap into reinforcements.
- **Energy:** Inverted crown chakra—void, violet, and deep black; disconnection from the divine cosmos through cold mechanical will.
- **Visual semiotics:** Static, broken characters, and terminal corruption. They appear as literal software errors and glitching geometric shapes, completely rejecting traditional vehicle or humanoid forms.
  - *ASCII examples:* `%`, `&`, `?`, `$`, `~`, `#`. An advancing swarm might look like `&%#`, reading less like an army and more like a creeping segmentation fault.
- **References:** The hive mind, digital lich, Borg, computer virus, and inevitable undead.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Citizens:** Spare parts awaiting assimilation.
  - **Ravels:** Generous foes who leave useful scrap.
  - **Feudals:** Flesh is waste; gold armor can be recycled.
  - **Alder:** An anomaly whose organic dead leave too little metal for the grave.

### 8.4 Feudals

- **Concept:** A tormented three-species caste system rebuilding their shattered empire in exile. Long ago, human encounters exposed the fragility of their divine hierarchy. Driven to the outer rings, they discovered the ancient Nexuses and forged a new, ruthless religion of ultimate order. The delicate Duos command, the Clerics orchestrate the dogma, and the hulking Submitters bear the physical weight of the empire. Order is the only truth, and order demands blood.
- **Philosophy:** Machiavellianism and divine right. Order demands sacrifice; lower castes have a sacred duty to preserve the elite.
- **Strengths:** Elite artillery, defensive auras, and punishing chokepoint control through synergy between protected command pieces and living shields.
- **Energy:** Solar plexus chakra—willpower, gold, authority, ego, and dominion. Royal purple, gold, and blood red.
- **Visual semiotics:** Heavily stratified layouts, pyramids, and thrones. Their shapes rely on massive, blocky characters shielding ornate, delicate symbols, visually representing their sacrificial caste system.
  - *ASCII examples:* `V`, `M`, `W`, `U`, protecting fragile cores like `*`, `o`, `^`. A Feudal formation might read as `V*V`, where the heavy outer letters exist solely to absorb damage for the center.
- **References:** The demiurge, fallen angels, the Covenant, and absolute monarchy.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Citizens:** Blasphemers who shattered our first paradise.
  - **Ravels:** The chaos we are sworn to extinguish.
  - **Glitch:** Soulless mockeries of sacred machinery.
  - **Alder:** Elegant fools who refuse the divine power inside the core.

### 8.5 Alder

- **Concept:** An ancient species of lean, hyper-advanced beings who distill simplicity down to its absolute essence. Their machines are tall, sleek, and practically magical. Seeing the Nexuses as crude and dangerous artifacts, they stubbornly refuse to draw power from the cores, relying entirely on their intrinsic superiority and ancient mastery. They bend the rules of the battlefield through pure elegance, striking with precision before fading back into the void.
- **Philosophy:** Transcendentalism and deep ecology. Organic perfection, self-reliance, and preservation of an elegant universe. Their refusal of Nexus power is also a claim of moral superiority.
- **Strengths:** Elite durability, intrinsic displacement, control, and powerful area healing.
- **Energy:** Third eye chakra—intuition, indigo, foresight, and ethereal light. Viridian, bioluminescent cyan, and pearl.
- **Visual semiotics:** Tall, elegant, and lean characters completely avoiding the mechanical blockiness of the lesser races. Their shapes rely on smooth verticality and delicate curves.
  - *ASCII examples:* `|`, `Y`, `(`, `)`, `l`, `i`. An elite Alder unit might look like `(Y)`, projecting a sense of sleek, contained, organic power.
- **References:** The high elf, hermit, Eldar, Protoss, and ancient sages watching empires rise and fall.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Citizens:** Blind children playing with an incomprehensible fire.
  - **Ravels:** Fleeting noise in the elegant void.
  - **Glitch:** A tragic corruption of life and machine that must be cleansed.
  - **Feudals:** Zealots worshipping a machine they should dismantle.

### 8.6 Faction design law

Every faction needs more than a skin. It requires:

- one central strategic **verb**;
- one philosophical **contradiction**;
- one restricted visual grammar;
- one characteristic base geometry;
- one recognizable motion and effect language;
- two or three Commanders representing disagreements inside the faction.

| Faction | Verb | Contradiction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Citizens | Build | Collective security can become imperial control |
| Ravels | Break free | Absolute freedom still depends on shared trust |
| Glitch | Recompile | Perfect continuity destroys individual identity |
| Feudals | Obey | Every caste disputes who embodies divine order |
| Alder | Transcend | Refusing dangerous power can become arrogant inaction |

The glyph examples define shape vocabulary, not exclusive global assignments. Exact battlefield mappings must preserve stable semantic roles, one-cell width, accessibility, and rapid recognition.

## 9. ASCII and visual language

The initial semantic grammar is:

- `@` for the Commander/Nexus Symbol;
- lowercase letters for common mobile actors;
- uppercase letters for structures and rare anchors;
- lines and blocks for terrain, walls, and construction boundaries;
- punctuation for resources, salvage, projectiles, sparks, and temporary effects;
- negative space for scale, silence, absence, and danger.

The simulation knows semantic IDs such as `unit.worker` and `structure.nexus`, never literal glyphs.

Good ASCII art follows these rules:

1. **Silhouette first.** A portrait remains readable when reduced mentally to its outline.
2. **Directional glyphs carry meaning.** `/`, `\`, `|`, `-`, brackets, and blocks imply different forces.
3. **Negative space is material.** It describes cavities, distance, elegance, wounds, and impossible absence.
4. **Limit local color.** Most subjects need a primary color and one highlight; more colors should convey state or material.
5. **Reserve visual weight.** Inverse cells, bold blocks, and full-screen shifts belong to rare authority and catastrophe.
6. **Motion completes the drawing.** Anticipation, trail, recoil, debris, settling, and formation give weight to a one-cell actor.
7. **Meaning beats fidelity.** Portrait and glyph should rhyme, not reproduce the same anatomy.
8. **Fresh-eye tests are mandatory.** Familiarity can hide an unreadable symbol.

Faction aesthetics apply to shapes, movement, placement, attacks, interface accents, portraits, and prose. Citizens are load-bearing and rectilinear. Ravels are diagonal, improvised, and slightly mistimed. Glitch violates stable terminal conventions. Feudals stratify marks into protected ranks and halos. Alder uses sparse, elongated geometry and controlled displacement.

Strong full-screen glitches are reserved for Nexus authority, Glitch identity, Originals, Commander restoration, and catastrophic destruction. Ordinary weapons need distinct physical languages so every event does not become the same computer effect.

A faction whose identity is illegibility lives under one rule, so that identity and readability can both survive: **corruption is drawn in the `effects` band and above, never in `units` or `structures`. It may add, overdraw, and unsettle; it may never remove or replace the only cell carrying a required semantic cue.** The screen may look wrong. The player must still be able to see what is attacking them. See [`engine.md`](engine.md) Section 10.4 and Q4 in [`open-questions.md`](open-questions.md).

Useful artistic precedents include NetHack's semantic glyphs and inspection, Brogue's restrained lighting and terrain, Dwarf Fortress's accumulation of simulated meaning, and Cogmind's modern science-fiction ASCII interface and procedural effects. These are references, not templates.

[`ascii-art-references.md`](ascii-art-references.md) collects those sources with what each one is actually good for, alongside drawing tools and the readability lessons the roguelike community has already paid for. It is research, not authority — when it disagrees with this section, this section wins.

## 10. Voice and lore economy

Moment-to-moment writing combines sincere wonder with dry terminal humor. Humor comes from institutional composure rather than constant references to shell commands:

```text
ANNEX ZERO EVACUATION COMPLETE
ANNEX ZERO NO LONGER LOCATED
```

Programming and shell references should be rare and subtle. System text explains rules, dialogue establishes motive, ASCII composition communicates scale, and effects communicate the kind of power acting.

Ordinary campaign targets:

- briefing: 120–180 words;
- pre-battle exchange: no more than three short lines;
- bark: 3–8 words;
- mid-mission interruption: one sentence unless play pauses;
- debrief: 80–120 words;
- optional artifact entry: 30–80 words.

A mission should teach one major mechanic, change one relationship, answer one local question, and open one larger mystery.

## 11. Existing story seeds

These summaries preserve useful characters and themes without making every detail mandatory campaign history.

### Nothing to Declare

Ravel scavenger Pella Vey crashes into an Ancient transit sanctuary while fleeing Glitch. Inside, a damaged Glitch subprocess carries stolen memories and an authority wafer. Pella exploits the sanctuary's category of **Witness** to help it exist outside the Queen's ownership. It joins her ship under the single glyph `?`. The story establishes that Ravel identity can be chosen, Ancient protocols contain forgotten legal possibilities, and a Glitch process can become contradictory enough to become a person.

### The Shape of an Open Hand

Ilyr, an Alder defector, joins a mismatched Ravel crew attacking a Feudal cargo convoy and freeing enslaved Submitters. When Feudal Priests restore control through healing and pain, Ilyr refuses both Alder detachment and direct mind control. He rewires the hierarchy into a circle in which Submitters sustain one another. The story establishes the Ravels' strength as their capacity to make room for what someone becomes next, while exposing the cruelty hidden inside Alder perfection and Feudal order.

These stories are models for Terminal Nexus fiction: a small mission, one concrete mechanism, several incompatible philosophies, dry humor under pressure, and a local choice that reveals the galaxy without explaining it.
