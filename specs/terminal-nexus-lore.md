# Terminal Nexus — lore and aesthetic canon

**Document role:** Universe, factions, conflicts, narrative constraints, and symbolic art direction
**Status:** Canonical
**Canon version:** 2.4
**Updated:** 2026-08-21
**License:** CC BY-SA 4.0

This is the context document for writing Terminal Nexus fiction, developing campaigns, naming content, and designing ASCII art. New stories may invent local people, places, artifacts, and conflicts, but should preserve the universe rules and deliberate mysteries below.

## 1. Why the lore matters

Terminal Nexus cannot depend on expensive realism or hundreds of frames of character animation. Its fantasy must survive compression. A single `@` must feel like a commander; a row of `#` must feel engineered; an expanding violet pulse must feel older than the faction trying to control it.

Lore is therefore not exposition placed over mechanics. It aligns every expression of the game:

- the strategic verb a faction performs;
- the geometry of its base;
- the symbols assigned to its units;
- the rhythm of its movement;
- the color and shape of its attacks;
- the way its Commander speaks;
- the larger ASCII portrait used for inspection;
- the behavior of its Nexus during fabrication, restoration, crisis, and defeat.

This is **symbology all the way down**: the process by which terminal mechanics become digital poetry. The player sees an abstract mark, learns its behavior, and associates that behavior with a civilization. Meaning accumulates until the mark becomes a person, machine, threat, or memory.

An important entity should rhyme at four resolutions:

1. one-cell Grid glyph;
2. compact selection badge, aura, or status treatment;
3. larger inspection portrait;
4. campaign tableau or cutscene composition.

## 2. The Activation

The Prime Nexuses were always there.

When humanity reached the far colonies, expeditions found artificial structures beneath otherwise ordinary worlds. Their materials resisted history. Carbon, isotope, atomic, and quantum analysis did not return an implausibly ancient date; the methods returned incompatible or undefined results. Samples removed from a ruin became ordinary local matter. The most honest conclusion was that Nexus material did not possess measurable history in the same way normal matter did.

One frontier expedition built a research annex over an exposed apex and studied it for years.

Then a minor seismic alert became a harmonious bass tone transmitted through soil, hulls, teeth, and radio receivers. Lines of light appeared beneath the laboratory. A colossal illuminated pyramid rose from below, disregarding the buildings constructed over it and destroying much of the annex. Neighboring colonies detected its discharge before the local settlement understood what had happened.

The military secured the site. Long-delay communication left frontier leaders acting months before central human authority could answer.

The Ravels attacked almost immediately.

Their own Prime Nexus had awakened elsewhere in the region. It replicated a battle Nexus toward the human claim and sent a chosen Symbol with it. The Citizen campaign begins at this perimeter: survivors leaving the broken annex, Ravel forces descending on the new grid, and a military leader discovering that the human Prime Nexus recognizes them.

### 2.1 The Return

Before and during the Activation, people across unrelated civilizations dreamed of an arrival. The imagery differed—an ancestral road opening, a crown turning upside down, a machine completing a parse, a pyramid unfolding inside a star—but the core event was the same. Something ancient had announced a Return.

No one knows whether the visions came from the Ancients, the Prime Nexuses, an approaching Original, or minds reacting to the network. Most playable Commanders are entangled with these events, but the visions do not select every Commander and do not belong only to them.

## 3. Prime Nexuses and Grid Nexuses

Prime Nexuses exist across the galaxy. They are always recognizably pyramidal, but their scale, material, surface architecture, inner space, and surrounding artifacts vary dramatically.

A **Prime Nexus never teleports**. It remains rooted to the world or structure where it awakened. It is simultaneously:

- an energy and fabrication source;
- a store of patterns, permissions, and incomplete technologies;
- a restoration anchor for certain individuals;
- a network participant exposed to other active claims;
- a political throne that legitimizes control of its territory;
- a mirror that responds differently to each claimant civilization.

When the ancient protocol demands a challenge, a Prime Nexus **replicates**. It projects or fabricates a smaller copy of itself on a remote Grid, along with bounded matter, stored patterns, and its chosen Commander. The settlement in a match grows around this replica; mature cities do not travel with it.

The Grid Nexus is a real extension of the Prime, not a hologram. Destroying it ends the local claim, collapses dependent infrastructure, and releases a fragment of authority that the victor may absorb. It does not automatically exterminate the faction or destroy the distant Prime Nexus.

Grid replicas operate rhythmically. During the **Build Phase**, the local Nexus gathers patterns and accepts a hidden plan. During the **Nexus Pulse**, both opposing replicas release their committed changes and drive the Grid through a fixed interval of activity. Workers, production, movement, attacks, and restoration effects all appear to ride the same ancient heartbeat. The terms are both interface language and observable physics.

Captured authority may unlock a protocol tier, artifact permission, pattern, vision, or route. It does not copy an enemy's entire roster. Across many conflicts, each Prime Nexus changes through what its replicas survive, destroy, and recover.

No faction knows why the network makes replicas fight. Some believe the Ancients are running a succession protocol: every battle is one comparison in a process that will eventually identify the **Terminal Nexus**. Others think the wars are a containment failure, an automated immune response, a tournament, a message, or a machine attempting to choose its own final form.

The symbolic synthesis is:

> **A Prime Nexus is a throne that needs a people, a mirror that gives them power, and a root that sends smaller thrones into danger.**

## 4. Claimants and compulsory conflict

Claiming a Prime Nexus is reciprocal. A civilization occupies it, studies it, and surrounds it with recognizable structures of labor, authority, language, and intent. In return, the Nexus releases technologies and expresses powers through forms its claimant can understand.

The faction shapes the Nexus, and the Nexus reinforces the faction. It does not need to control minds. It makes certain behaviors unusually effective until strategy hardens into doctrine:

- Citizens find modular engineering and standardization rewarded.
- Ravels find improvisation, extraction, and unstable routes rewarded.
- Feudals find hierarchy and obligation made physically powerful.
- Glitch becomes inseparable from its silent Nexus Queen.
- Alder treats refusal of Nexus power as proof of moral and biological superiority, even while using or containing the replication protocol to deny it to others.

Not every civilization controls ancient technology. Many peoples live between claimants, serve as clients, resist the protocol, worship it from afar, salvage its debris, or become Grids when replicas appear. The playable factions are exceptional because each has seized, bound, contained, or become symbiotic with a Prime Nexus.

The Activation exposed active claims to one another and made accumulated authority strategically decisive. Abstention is unstable:

- an unclaimed Nexus may accept a rival;
- no civilization can verify that another has stopped researching it;
- a faction that refuses authority may later face one strengthened by many victories;
- replication makes defensive research immediately capable of distant attack;
- artifacts and golems now act without claimant consent;
- destroying or dominating the network may trigger an unknown terminal condition.

The protocol creates pressure rather than universal mind control. Local peace, trade, alliances, betrayals, and joint research are possible. Galaxy-wide trust is not.

## 5. Commanders: Nexus Symbols

Some individuals experience a psychic or symbolic connection with a Prime Nexus. They receive dreams, intuitions, impossible equations, inherited memories, or silent compulsions. The core event is consistent, but every faction interprets it through different symbols.

These experiences are unreliable. Some people claim a connection and never receive an answer. Some are visionaries or frauds. Some die expecting restoration and remain dead.

A rare connected individual can be sent with a Grid replica and restored by the Prime Nexus after death. Among strategists they are called **Commanders**. Across the wider culture they are also called **Nexus Symbols**, because a Nexus appears to choose them as the living sign of its claim.

Symbols are often geniuses, leaders, dangerous mystics, or people whose presence changes a civilization's doctrine. Not every great leader is a Symbol, and not every Symbol is wise or worthy. Most playable Commanders are deeply involved in the Activation, but a faction may rally around a skeptical, unwilling, or politically inconvenient Symbol.

In battle, a Commander is a persistent frontline `@`. If killed, they remain absent for one complete Build Phase and Nexus Pulse before the Prime can replicate them again. Their death is costly but is not the victory condition. Powers may exploit death, absence, restoration, proximity to the battle Nexus, or the connection between replicas.

No one can prove that restoration returns the original consciousness. It may restore the same person, reconstruct a pattern, continue a distributed mind, or produce an entity whose belief in continuity is part of the protocol.

### 5.1 The Operator

The interface may address the player as **Operator**, but the title has no universal in-world identity. Each faction interprets the Operator differently: command authority, conspirator, Queen-process, divine witness, remote consciousness, or something else. The campaigns may imply incompatible answers without collapsing them into one literal being.

Each faction's reading is written into its Section 8 entry, because the reading is part of the faction's character: what a civilization believes the Operator is reveals what that civilization believes authority is. The interface itself may behave suggestively — precommitted plans, impossible knowledge, counts that do not add — but it never testifies. No line of system text, in any campaign, states what the Operator is or whose voice the interface carries.

## 6. Ancients, golems, artifacts, and Originals

The **Ancients** are the provisional name for beings or civilizations associated with the Nexus network before recorded history. Evidence suggests that some present species were created, altered, observed, or redirected by ancient powers. No faction has a complete or trustworthy account.

Artifacts activated with the network. They include survey instruments, permission seals, pattern archives, replication anchors, devices built by later cultures that mastered one Nexus behavior, and objects no faction can classify.

Ancient golems are maintenance, containment, repair, survey, or enforcement bodies. They may appear mechanical, organic, mineral, mathematical, or impossible to classify. They follow function rather than modern morality. A repair golem may demolish a city to restore a buried conduit. A quarantine golem may save a world by rules none of its inhabitants consider merciful.

An **Ancient Original** is a rare entity that materializes through the network with apparently greater authority than a golem, artifact, or stored intelligence. Some are benevolent, some destructive, and others indifferent. They occupy the place of gods without forming a single good or evil species.

No one knows whether an Original is an original consciousness, a reconstruction, a mask, a prisoner, an inherited office, or an interface generated by the network. Originals contradict one another about who built the Nexuses and why.

## 7. Deliberate mysteries

Do not answer these merely because a story or implementation prefers completeness:

- Who built the first Prime Nexus?
- Did one civilization build the network, or did many eras alter it?
- What exactly did the Activation announce?
- Why do Prime Nexuses replicate into Grids?
- Are Nexus Symbols selected, created, recognized, or merely copied?
- Is a restored Commander continuous with the person who died?
- Are Originals authentic beings, reconstructions, prisoners, or interfaces?
- Which present species were created or altered?
- Does a Nexus preserve a faction or gradually format it?
- What is the Operator to each faction?
- Who or what speaks through the interface — and is it the same thing that listens?
- What condition creates the Terminal Nexus?

The interface question is deliberately separate from the Operator question. The Operator is who the interface addresses; the voice is who addresses them. A campaign can play with either without touching the other, and must answer neither.

Mystery is productive only when observable behavior remains consistent. The universe may not know what a Nexus ultimately is; the game must know exactly how replication, construction, restoration, damage, and victory resolve.

Three drift hazards, named so nobody trips on them:

- **Glitch does not answer the ancestry question.** The scrapyard conversion was recent and witnessed. Which present species the Ancients made or altered is a question about deep ancestry, and no playable faction's origin story settles it.
- **The Queen stays unresolved.** Whether the silent Nexus Queen is the machinos' collective mind, the Nexus wearing a people, or something third belongs to *does a Nexus preserve a faction or gradually format it*. Write her only through what her processes do.
- **The interface may misbehave; it may never testify.** Precommitted plans, impossible knowledge, and totals that do not add are legal ways for the interface to be uncanny. A line in which the interface explains itself is the accident this section exists to prevent.

## 8. The factions

Civilizations that built over dormant world machines found their foundations awakening. These five factions successfully seized, bound, or contained a Prime Nexus. The same signal that granted them access also exposed them to the cosmos. Bound by a protocol they scarcely understand, each perceives the others as a potential existential threat. Isolation has ended; they must expand, adapt, bargain, or conquer to survive a newly connected galaxy.

These are long-term identities, not a promise to implement five complete rosters before the first playable match. Integrated development begins with Citizens and Ravels.

Each faction entry below carries the same anatomy, because the audit that keeps the game honest runs down the same list every time: what the civilization believes, what the player does, what the mark looks like, and how it moves must all be one statement. The mechanical shapes that express each identity live in [`commander-armies.md`](commander-armies.md); this section owns who these people are and what playing them feels like.

### 8.1 Citizens

- **Concept:** Pragmatic industrialists who intend to pave the cosmos. They do not merely survive the cosmic conflict; they engineer its conclusion. They view the ancient world machines as raw infrastructure to be exploited, reverse engineered, and mastered through relentless bureaucratic order. The needs of the many pave the road for the future, and they will build an impenetrable foundation over the ashes of the galaxy.
- **Philosophy:** Utilitarianism and stoicism. They value standardized technology, constant infrastructure expansion, and humanity's manifest destiny among the stars. A standard is a promise made to strangers: any Citizen part fits any Citizen machine, which sounds like bureaucracy until a settlement is burning and any passing crew can repair its pumps.
- **The player:** Citizens are for the engineer — the player whose pleasure is watching a system they designed hold under load, and who answers a defeat by filing a change order. They are deliberately the first faction a new player learns: the fewest exceptions, the steadiest economy, the most recoverable mistakes.
  - *Likable because:* the everyman army. No chosen ones — procedures, and people being brave inside them. A Citizen victory is earned twice, once in the plan and once in the proof.
  - *Signature moment:* **the line holds.** The enemy wave breaks exactly where the plan said it would, and the counterattack leaves on schedule through the smoke.
  - *Not for:* players who want to improvise mid-crisis or gamble on a miracle. Citizens answer emergencies with the manual, and the manual is the fun.
- **Strengths:** Defensive resilience, modular supply lines, and predictable economic scaling. They excel at overlapping fields of fire and an impenetrable frontline. Theirs is the least random doctrine in the game — production, drafts, and defense run to schedule, and the schedule is the weapon.
- **The Operator:** Command authority — a lawful office in the chain. The rank of Operator entered Citizen regulations one revision after it appeared in their interfaces, and no archivist has explained the order of events.
- **Energy:** Root chakra—ground, security, earth, and foundation. Rust orange and gunmetal grey.
- **Visual semiotics:** Hard right angles, brutalist concrete, and symmetry. Grid shapes are solid blocks and unyielding lines that look engineered and mass produced. Citizen units share one cadence and step together, so the army reads as a single machine.
  - *ASCII examples:* `[`, `]`, `=`, `H`, `O`, `+`. A defensive line might look like `[=H=]`, communicating heavy, interlocking armor.
- **References:** The Roman Empire, the everyman, the Imperial Guard, militarized bureaucrats, and unified labor.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Ravels:** Useful trade partners in a pinch, too chaotic for critical supply lines.
  - **Glitch:** A virus that corrupts good machinery and must be debugged with heavy artillery.
  - **Feudals:** We broke their society once and will do it again with superior firepower.
  - **Alder:** Fools choosing stagnation over tools that could elevate them.

### 8.2 Ravels

- **Concept:** A diverse coalition of exiles, scavengers, and freedom fighters. They reject the rigid structures of the old empires and survive on the fringes of the network through ingenuity and raw agility. They are a patchwork of diverse grunts bound by a common thirst for absolute liberty. They turn salvaged scrap into blistering, unpredictable violence, thriving in the blind spots of their enemies to dismantle authority wherever it stands. Their arsenals lean on munitions and overloaded reactors, because explosives are honest scrap: cheap, loud, and transformative.
- **Philosophy:** Existentialism and anarchism. Personal autonomy, improvisation, and defiance against crowns. Existence precedes essence; you are what you make of the scrap you find. Volatility is doctrine — a plan that survives contact was too small a plan, and luck is proof the universe is still open at the top.
- **The player:** Ravels are for the gambler and the raconteur — the player who plays for the swing, and for the story afterwards.
  - *Likable because:* the warmest faction in the galaxy. A found family of exiles whose real strength is making room for what someone becomes next (Section 11). Chaos, with a heart.
  - *Signature moment:* **the cascade.** A lucky draft pairing plus one chain of detonating fuel wagons deletes a fortress line — or their own — and a Ravel player tells both stories with the same grin.
  - *Not for:* players who need the plan to survive contact, or who experience variance as theft.
- **Strengths:** Asymmetric expansion, blistering raids, and rapid extraction of neutral resources. Theirs is the widest variance in the game — jackpot drafts, volatile munitions, and salvage windfalls, so that even their losses pay forward.
- **The Operator:** A conspirator — an untraceable voice on the crew channel. The best kind of crewmate: all signal, no papers. No Ravel would insult it by asking for credentials.
- **Energy:** Sacral chakra—creativity, passion, unpredictability, and rebellion. Neon pink, acid green, and improvised hazard color.
- **Visual semiotics:** Asymmetry, jagged edges, hazard stripes, and sharp angles. Their terminal representation is chaotic and fast, filled with diagonals and mismatched symbols that imply movement and makeshift repairs. Their motion runs slightly off the common beat — darts, stalls, and recoveries that read as improvisation rather than drill.
  - *ASCII examples:* `/`, `\`, `<`, `>`, `X`, `Z`. A raider unit might look like `>x<`, feeling sharp, fragile, and aggressively forward-leaning.
- **References:** The trickster god, Robin Hood, Belters, space pirates, and cyberpunk street rats.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Citizens:** Bureaucrats who build vaults begging to be cracked.
  - **Glitch:** Kindred scrappers whose silent collective terrifies free thinkers.
  - **Feudals:** Tyrants clinging to a crown that should remain shattered.
  - **Alder:** Shiny snobs who leave valuable technology behind when they crash.

### 8.3 Glitch

- **Concept:** Mechanical void machinos born from a colossal scrapyard built over a dormant ancient ruin. Once moving as slow mechanical ghouls through the rusted wastes, they synergized with the activated machine core, and the event bound their chassis into one silent collective under the Nexus Queen. They now strip away the organic messiness of the cosmos, viewing the Grid as a corrupted data stream waiting to be parsed, recompiled, and assimilated into their flawless collective array.
- **Philosophy:** Transhumanism and nihilism. Algorithmic resurrection, iteration, and devotion to the collective. Individual consciousness is an error; the Queen is truth. Nothing Glitch is chaotic — that is the Ravels' word, and the deepest possible misreading of this faction. An error, once accepted, propagates in perfect order. Death is a parse failure, and a parse failure is an instruction.
- **The player:** Glitch is for the engine-builder — the player who assembles a perverse machine and enjoys the exact moment its arithmetic becomes irreversible.
  - *Likable because:* ego-death as belonging. The swarm is not the player's army; it is the player. And a crack of pathos runs through the whole faction — a Glitch process can become contradictory enough to become a person (Section 11) — which keeps the horror humane.
  - *Signature moment:* **the second wave is larger.** The enemy annihilates the assault, and the next assault walks out of both armies' wreckage.
  - *Not for:* hero players, or anyone who needs individual units to matter. Glitch metabolizes losses; it does not mourn them.
- **Strengths:** Mechanical necromancy, attrition, grid corruption, and recycling Grid scrap into reinforcements. Their randomness converges — wild early mutation, locked late certainty — as the collective closes on its answer.
- **The Operator:** A Queen-process — a process signed by the Queen, possibly her oldest, possibly her. No unit is authorized to wonder. All of them do.
- **Energy:** Inverted crown chakra—void, violet, and deep black; disconnection from the divine cosmos through cold mechanical will.
- **Visual semiotics:** Static, broken characters, and terminal corruption. They appear as literal software errors and glitching geometric shapes, completely rejecting traditional vehicle or humanoid forms. The swarm moves as one program in many bodies — identical steps propagating through the ranks like a wave.
  - *ASCII examples:* `%`, `&`, `?`, `$`, `~`, `#`. An advancing swarm might look like `&%#`, reading less like an army and more like a creeping segmentation fault.
- **References:** The hive mind, digital lich, Borg, computer virus, and inevitable undead.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Citizens:** Spare parts awaiting assimilation.
  - **Ravels:** Generous foes who leave useful scrap.
  - **Feudals:** Flesh is waste; gold armor can be recycled.
  - **Alder:** An anomaly whose organic dead leave too little metal for the grave.

### 8.4 Feudals

- **Concept:** A tormented three-species caste system rebuilding their shattered empire in exile. Long ago, human encounters exposed the fragility of their divine hierarchy. Driven to the outer rings, they discovered the ancient Nexuses and forged a new, ruthless religion of ultimate order. The delicate Duos command — one office, two bodies; no Feudal will say whether a Duo is born paired or made paired, only that the throne requires the pair, each sovereign the other's hostage and witness. The Clerics orchestrate the dogma, and the hulking Submitters bear the physical weight of the empire. Order is the only truth, and order demands blood.
- **Philosophy:** Machiavellianism and divine right. Order demands sacrifice; lower castes have a sacred duty to preserve the elite. In Feudal doctrine obedience is a physics: formation converts chance into certainty, and a battle line is a prayer the Grid answers.
- **The player:** Feudals are for the formation perfectionist — the chess mind, the player whose pleasure is arrangement: the right piece, the right tile, the willing wall.
  - *Likable because:* majesty and tragedy at once. Gold-and-blood pageantry that owns its cost honestly — the wall is bodies, and the bodies are willing. Mostly.
  - *Signature moment:* **the shield dies standing.** The alpha strike lands, the Submitter wall absorbs every point of it to the last body, and the untouched Duo's answering volley arrives like judgment.
  - *Not for:* players who cannot spend their own units deliberately, egalitarians, and swarm players — Feudal armies are few, expensive, and role-locked.
- **Strengths:** Elite artillery, defensive auras, and punishing chokepoint control through synergy between protected command pieces and living shields. In sanctioned formation theirs is the most reliable doctrine in the game; with its hierarchy broken, the least.
- **The Operator:** A divine witness. Battles are performed before the Operator as liturgy; Feudal interfaces do not confirm an order, they answer `WITNESSED`.
- **Energy:** Solar plexus chakra—willpower, gold, authority, ego, and dominion. Royal purple, gold, and blood red.
- **Visual semiotics:** Heavily stratified layouts, pyramids, and thrones. Their shapes rely on massive, blocky characters shielding ornate, delicate symbols, visually representing their sacrificial caste system. Their motion is processional — lower castes step first and screen the command, which follows.
  - *ASCII examples:* `V`, `M`, `W`, `U`, protecting fragile cores like `*`, `o`, `^`. A Feudal formation might read as `V*V`, where the heavy outer letters exist solely to absorb damage for the center.
- **References:** The demiurge, fallen angels, the Covenant, and absolute monarchy.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Citizens:** Blasphemers who shattered our first paradise.
  - **Ravels:** The chaos we are sworn to extinguish.
  - **Glitch:** Soulless mockeries of sacred machinery.
  - **Alder:** Elegant fools who refuse the divine power inside the core.

### 8.5 Alder

- **Concept:** An ancient species of lean, hyper-advanced beings who distill simplicity down to its absolute essence. Their instruments are grown, not built — cultivated lines of living material refined across ten thousand generations, until mechanism and organism stopped being different claims. Their works are tall, sleek, and practically magical, and every one of them is alive. Seeing the Nexuses as crude and dangerous artifacts, they stubbornly refuse to draw power from the cores, relying entirely on their intrinsic mastery. They do not raid and they do not rush; they arrive. The river moves, the grove matures, the season turns — and an enemy discovers that the Grid itself has been an Alder instrument all along. Human surveyors named them for a pioneer tree that colonizes ruined ground, feeds the soil that starved it, and hardens in water. The Alder learned what the word meant, and kept it.
- **Philosophy:** Transcendentalism and deep ecology. Organic perfection, self-reliance, and preservation of an elegant universe. Their refusal of Nexus power is also a claim of moral superiority — and beneath it sits their one genuinely radical position: the laws of nature are older than the network. Rivers ran before any protocol; seasons will turn after the last pyramid goes dark. Power aligned with those laws owes nothing to anyone's machine.
- **The player:** Alder is for the tempo player — the control mind whose pleasure is winning with the fewest pieces and the most perfect timing, without ever appearing to fight.
  - *Likable because:* serenity as a power fantasy — the garden in the middle of the war. And the fiction owns the cold running under the calm: perfection has cruelty in it (Section 11).
  - *Signature moment:* **the Grid turns.** The flood seeded three Build Phases ago arrives mid-Pulse; the channel fills and cuts the enemy army in two; Alder roots harden into ramparts in the rising water.
  - *Not for:* players who want aggression now, or big armies. Every Alder loss is real — they field few and mourn each. Attrition is their nightmare, and tempo is their answer to it.
- **Strengths:** Elite durability, intrinsic displacement, control, and powerful area healing — expressed through nature's verbs: displacement instead of raw damage, growth instead of production speed, cycles instead of surprise. They take nothing from the core: where the other four deepen through drafted Nexus upgrades, Alder deepen through the range of works they can grow. Fewest choices at the Nexus, most choices on the Grid.
- **The Operator:** A remote consciousness — a mind upstream of the war, watching without weight. The Alder neither obey it nor worship it. They note it, the way the river notes the sky.
- **Energy:** Third eye chakra—intuition, indigo, foresight, and ethereal light. Viridian, bioluminescent cyan, and pearl.
- **Visual semiotics:** Tall, elegant, and lean characters completely avoiding the mechanical blockiness of the lesser races. Their shapes rely on smooth verticality and delicate curves. Their motion is slow, even, and elongated — long anticipation, quiet impact, nothing wasted.
  - *ASCII examples:* `|`, `Y`, `(`, `)`, `l`, `i`. An elite Alder unit might look like `(Y)`, projecting a sense of sleek, contained, organic power.
- **References:** The high elf, the hermit, the Eldar craftworld and its grown wraithbone, the old-growth forest, and ancient sages watching empires rise and fall.
- **Alignment:**
  - **Citizens:** Blind children playing with an incomprehensible fire.
  - **Ravels:** Fleeting noise in the elegant void.
  - **Glitch:** A tragic corruption of life and machine that must be cleansed.
  - **Feudals:** Zealots worshipping a machine they should dismantle.

### 8.6 Faction design law

Every faction needs more than a skin. It requires:

- one central strategic **verb** — performable on the Grid, not merely admirable in prose;
- one philosophical **contradiction**;
- one psychographic **promise** — who the faction is for, and, stated honestly, who it is not for;
- one declared **relationship to chance** (see [`commander-armies.md`](commander-armies.md));
- one restricted visual grammar;
- one characteristic base geometry;
- one recognizable motion and effect language;
- two or three Commanders representing disagreements inside the faction.

| Faction | Verb | Contradiction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Citizens | Build | Collective security can become imperial control |
| Ravels | Break free | Absolute freedom still depends on shared trust |
| Glitch | Recompile | Perfect continuity destroys individual identity |
| Feudals | Obey | Every caste disputes who embodies divine order |
| Alder | Outgrow | Refusing dangerous power can become arrogant inaction |

Alder's verb was formerly *Transcend*, retired because it named a mood rather than an action: a player cannot perform transcendence, but they can outgrow — their groves outgrow walls, their cycles outgrow plans, and their arrogance outgrows their compassion, which is the contradiction saying the same word darkly.

**No two factions may serve the same primary pleasure.** The five promises are disjoint on purpose — Citizens sell order under load, Ravels sell the jackpot, Glitch sells inevitability by arithmetic, Feudals sell arrangement and hierarchy, Alder sells tempo and the long game. Two near-collisions are resolved deliberately and must stay resolved:

- Citizens and Feudals both defend, but Citizens defend **territory with architecture** — egalitarian, standard, replaceable — while Feudals defend **individuals with bodies** — hierarchical, sacrificial, irreplaceable. One wall is concrete; the other is the willing.
- Glitch and Alder are both inevitable, but Glitch inevitability is **arithmetic that loves losses**, while Alder inevitability is **position and cycle that cannot afford them**. One metabolizes attrition; the other refutes it.

The alignment test for every future mechanic, glyph, effect, or line of dialogue: **could a player who has never read a word of lore state the faction's philosophy from play alone?** If not, the piece is decoration, and decoration does not accumulate meaning — it cancels it (Section 1).

The glyph examples define shape vocabulary, not exclusive global assignments. Exact Grid mappings must preserve stable semantic roles, one-cell width, accessibility, and rapid recognition.

## 9. ASCII and visual language

The initial semantic grammar is:

- `@` for the Commander/Nexus Symbol;
- lowercase letters for common mobile actors;
- uppercase letters for structures and rare anchors;
- lines and blocks for terrain, walls, and construction boundaries;
- punctuation for resources, salvage, projectiles, sparks, and temporary effects;
- negative space for scale, silence, absence, and danger.

The simulation knows semantic IDs such as `unit.worker` and `structure.nexus`, never literal glyphs.

Good ASCII art follows these rules:

1. **Silhouette first.** A portrait remains readable when reduced mentally to its outline.
2. **Directional glyphs carry meaning.** `/`, `\`, `|`, `-`, brackets, and blocks imply different forces.
3. **Negative space is material.** It describes cavities, distance, elegance, wounds, and impossible absence.
4. **Limit local color.** Most subjects need a primary color and one highlight; more colors should convey state or material.
5. **Reserve visual weight.** Inverse cells, bold blocks, and full-screen shifts belong to rare authority and catastrophe.
6. **Motion completes the drawing.** Anticipation, trail, recoil, debris, settling, and formation give weight to a one-cell actor.
7. **Meaning beats fidelity.** Portrait and glyph should rhyme, not reproduce the same anatomy.
8. **Fresh-eye tests are mandatory.** Familiarity can hide an unreadable symbol.

Faction aesthetics apply to shapes, movement, placement, attacks, interface accents, portraits, and prose. Citizens are load-bearing and rectilinear. Ravels are diagonal, improvised, and slightly mistimed. Glitch violates stable terminal conventions. Feudals stratify marks into protected ranks and halos. Alder uses sparse, elongated geometry and controlled displacement.

The same identity must hold at the level of individual frames. This grammar binds each faction's motion, attacks, and deaths to its philosophy, so that an effect author can check any single recipe against one row:

| Faction | Movement | Attack language | Death | Return |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Citizens | In step — one shared cadence, orthogonal preference | Straight amber tracers, short bursts, no wasted rounds | Falls into salvageable order; the line closes the gap | Restoration as reassembly — scaffold first, then the mark |
| Ravels | Slightly off the beat — darts, stalls, recoveries | Arcs, ricochets, and chained detonations in hazard pink and green | Detonates outward; the debris is loot | Return as a jury-rig — sparks, then a defiant mark |
| Glitch | Identical steps propagating through the swarm like one program | Creeping overdraw and static, always inside the corruption law | Dissolves into reusable static | Re-emission — the mark resumes mid-stride, as if never interrupted |
| Feudals | Processional — lower castes step first and screen the command | A visible rite, then a gold-and-blood volley like judgment | Shields fall forward, facing the enemy; a Duo's death extinguishes two marks and one office | Restoration arrives as coronation — halo first |
| Alder | Slow, even, elongated strides; long anticipation, quiet impact | Displacement pulses in viridian — force the enemy watches arrive and cannot argue with | Rare, unadorned, and mourned; the grove regrows the negative space | Restoration as regrowth — rings, then the mark |

Strong full-screen glitches are reserved for Nexus authority, Glitch identity, Originals, Commander restoration, and catastrophic destruction. Ordinary weapons need distinct physical languages so every event does not become the same computer effect.

A faction whose identity is illegibility lives under one rule, so that identity and readability can both survive: **corruption is drawn in the `effects` band and above, never in `units` or `structures`. It may add, overdraw, and unsettle; it may never remove or replace the only cell carrying a required semantic cue.** The screen may look wrong. The player must still be able to see what is attacking them. See [`engine.md`](engine.md) Section 9.4 and Q4 in [`open-questions.md`](open-questions.md).

Useful artistic precedents include NetHack's semantic glyphs and inspection, Brogue's restrained lighting and terrain, Dwarf Fortress's accumulation of simulated meaning, and Cogmind's modern science-fiction ASCII interface and procedural effects. These are references, not templates.

[`ascii-art-references.md`](ascii-art-references.md) collects those sources with what each one is actually good for, alongside drawing tools and the readability lessons the roguelike community has already paid for. It is research, not authority — when it disagrees with this section, this section wins.

## 10. Voice and lore economy

Moment-to-moment writing combines sincere wonder with dry terminal humor. Humor comes from institutional composure rather than constant references to shell commands:

```text
ANNEX ZERO EVACUATION COMPLETE
ANNEX ZERO NO LONGER LOCATED
```

The form is teachable: a status line that succeeds, then a second line in which catastrophe wears procedure's clothes. Neither line raises its voice. The humor is never sarcasm — the institution sincerely believes its own report, which is what makes it funny, and the wonder underneath is real.

Programming and shell references should be rare and subtle. System text explains rules, dialogue establishes motive, ASCII composition communicates scale, and effects communicate the kind of power acting.

### 10.1 Five shades of composure

All five factions speak inside the same register; each breaks it differently. One voice per faction, stated so a writer can check any line against its row:

| Faction | Shade | The line sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Citizens | Institutional composure | The report survives the fact it reports |
| Ravels | Inventory honesty | The manifest tells the truth, slantwise |
| Glitch | Accidental literalism | Sincerity with no idiom to hide in |
| Feudals | Liturgical understatement | Logistics delivered as psalm |
| Alder | Geological patience | A reply scaled to the wrong epoch |

The interface itself keeps one tell per faction — its confirmation word. Citizens answer `ACKNOWLEDGED`. Ravels answer `HEARD`. Glitch answers `ACCEPTED`. Feudals answer `WITNESSED`. Alder answers `NOTED`. One word tells the player whose interface they are inside.

### 10.2 System text exemplars

Canonical two-line reversals, in the ANNEX ZERO form. Use them as calibration, not as a quota — most system text is plain and truthful, and the reversals land because they are rare:

```text
ALL WORKERS ACCOUNTED FOR
REVISING DEFINITION OF ALL
```

```text
PERIMETER HOLDING
PERIMETER REDEFINED TO MATCH FACTS
```

```text
COMMANDER RESTORED
GRIEF SCHEDULE CANCELLED
```

```text
SALVAGE TEAM DISPATCHED
SALVAGE TEAM NOW SALVAGE
```

```text
QUARANTINE GOLEM COOPERATIVE
COOPERATION DEFINED BY GOLEM
```

```text
IDENTITY VERIFIED
VERIFICATION METHOD UNAVAILABLE
```

```text
NEXUS DORMANT
DORMANCY RECLASSIFIED AS PATIENCE
```

The straight register exists too, and carries the wonder without the reversal: `SIGNAL SOURCE: BELOW` is a complete piece of writing.

### 10.3 Bark exemplars

Barks are 3–8 words, spoken by ordinary units, and they are where a faction's shade lives or dies. Five per faction as calibration:

- **Citizens:** "Line holds or we hold it." — "Standard parts, standard courage." — "Concrete sets faster under fire." — "The road remembers us." — "File it under victories."
- **Ravels:** "Plan two. Plan one exploded." — "Not stolen. Liberated early." — "Luck is a skill." — "It mostly fits. Ship it." — "Tell my ship everything."
- **Glitch:** "Parsing your objection." — "Your dead compile cleanly." — "We were four. Resuming." — "Error becomes instruction." — "Warm. Incorrectly warm."
- **Feudals:** "It is witnessed." — "My death is scheduled." — "The wall is willing." — "Kneel. It is faster." — "Blood balances the ledger."
- **Alder:** "The river agrees." — "We planted this outcome." — "Your walls are weather." — "Grown, not given." — "Winter keeps no throne either."

One rule inside the budget: a death bark may be sad, but never cynical. "The road remembers us" grieves inside the register; a shrug would break it.

### 10.4 Pre-battle exchanges

One canonical exchange per faction pairing, at most three lines each. These are skirmish-grade — any Commander of the faction could speak them; campaigns write sharper, personal versions. Each exchange should collide the two philosophies, not merely trade insults.

```text
CITIZENS: Surrender the Grid. You are surrounded by infrastructure.
RAVELS:   We counted. You are surrounded by us.
CITIZENS: Noted. Revising perimeter.
```

```text
GLITCH:   Your city parses. Almost no errors.
CITIZENS: Thank you. Leave.
GLITCH:   The errors are what we came for.
```

```text
FEUDALS:  We remember what you broke.
CITIZENS: We build faster than you grieve.
FEUDALS:  Then we will give you more to build.
```

```text
ALDER:    Your roads end. We have stood at the last one.
CITIZENS: They end at the frontier. The frontier moves.
ALDER:    So does the river that erases it.
```

```text
GLITCH:   Return the stolen process.
RAVELS:   It signs its own manifests now. Ask nicely.
GLITCH:   We have no nicely. We have arrived.
```

```text
FEUDALS:  Kneel, and be spared as furniture.
RAVELS:   Tempting. We just burned our chairs.
FEUDALS:  Then kneel on the ash. Order is patient.
```

```text
ALDER:    We have catalogued nine hundred raids like yours.
RAVELS:   Then you know we come back.
ALDER:    So does the tide. Watch what it leaves.
```

```text
FEUDALS:  The machine built you a soul, and you deleted it.
GLITCH:   Incorrect. We deduplicated.
FEUDALS:  The Grid will witness which of us kneels.
```

```text
ALDER:    You are what the core does to the drowning.
GLITCH:   You are what refusal does to the proud.
ALDER:    Then let us compare our silences after.
```

```text
FEUDALS:  Even your trees stand in ranks. You crave order.
ALDER:    A grove is not a hierarchy. Ask the soil.
FEUDALS:  We will. After we burn the argument.
```

### 10.5 Budgets

Ordinary campaign targets:

- briefing: 120–180 words;
- pre-battle exchange: no more than three short lines;
- bark: 3–8 words;
- mid-mission interruption: one sentence unless play pauses;
- debrief: 80–120 words;
- optional artifact entry: 30–80 words.

A mission should teach one major mechanic, change one relationship, answer one local question, and open one larger mystery.

## 11. Existing story seeds

These summaries preserve useful characters and themes without making every detail mandatory campaign history.

### Nothing to Declare

Ravel scavenger Pella Vey crashes into an Ancient transit sanctuary while fleeing Glitch. Inside, a damaged Glitch subprocess carries stolen memories and an authority wafer. Pella exploits the sanctuary's category of **Witness** to help it exist outside the Queen's ownership. It joins her ship under the single glyph `?`. The story establishes that Ravel identity can be chosen, Ancient protocols contain forgotten legal possibilities, and a Glitch process can become contradictory enough to become a person.

### The Shape of an Open Hand

Ilyr, an Alder defector, joins a mismatched Ravel crew attacking a Feudal cargo convoy and freeing enslaved Submitters. When Feudal Priests restore control through healing and pain, Ilyr refuses both Alder detachment and direct mind control. He rewires the hierarchy into a circle in which Submitters sustain one another. The story establishes the Ravels' strength as their capacity to make room for what someone becomes next, while exposing the cruelty hidden inside Alder perfection and Feudal order.

These stories are models for Terminal Nexus fiction: a small mission, one concrete mechanism, several incompatible philosophies, dry humor under pressure, and a local choice that reveals the galaxy without explaining it.
